Getting started

Your first week

LANES is one shared freight market. You run a carrier: you bid on loads, haul them, and grow from one leased truck into a fleet. Every other company is a player or an AI competing for the same freight.

You start as an owner-operator: one NPC-leased Workhorse with a dry van, 5,000 FD, and you behind the wheel. The lessor takes 15% of every load's pay and maintains the truck.

  • Day 1: open the Load board, pick a load near your truck, and bid. Bids clear at 06:00 and 18:00 game time.
  • Every day: your dispatcher (you, at first) bids for idle trucks you don't bid on by hand. Set its rules at the bottom of the Fleet tab.
  • First goal: save 18,000 FD for your own Workhorse and dry van. Owning ends the lessor's cut. See Buying trucks.
  • Then: more trucks, drivers to run them (Drivers), a dispatcher once you pass 3 trucks (Dispatchers).

New authority is on probation: some brokers won't load you until your authority is 30 days old, the strictest not before 90 days and a record. See Vetting.

The live world and test worlds

The live world is shared by every player and runs on the real clock: a game day every 6 real hours, a game year about every 91 real days. It never pauses, nothing resets, and every order stands. Your orders reach everyone and take effect about half a real minute later, at the same moment on every screen.

A test world is a private copy of the rules with only you and the AI in it. You can pause it and run it up to a game day every two seconds. On the public test site your test world is kept in your browser; starting a new one replaces it, and a test world from older rules can't be replayed.

Starts: the live world only offers the owner-operator start. A test world also offers a small fleet: two used trucks and 25,000 FD.

The screen and controls

  • The map shows every hub, the lanes between them, and the trucks on them: yours, other players', and the AI's. Tap a hub to see its open loads; tap your truck to select it.
  • The ticker under the clock lists what just happened to your company: loads won and delivered, breakdowns, inspections, staff news.
  • The tabs: Fleet (your trucks and the dispatcher), Load board, Staff, Dealer (new, used and leased trucks, facilities), Money (cash, loans, the exchange), Company (your record, the market report, the leaderboard), and this Guide.

The market

The load board

Every load shows a book-now price: take it and it's yours at once (Book-now prices). Everything else is a counter: you ask more than the posted price, and counters clear in rounds at 06:00 and 18:00. Each load has a ceiling, the most its poster wants to pay per mile; nobody sees anyone else's counter.

How a round clears

  • Every bid gets a score: 55% for price (how far under the ceiling), 25% for reputation (your on-time record), 20% for empty miles to the pickup (none is best; at 150 it scores zero and past that it counts against you).
  • Counters clear best score first. A truck wins at most one load a round, and a load goes to one truck.
  • A counter above the ceiling, up to 1.5× it, is taken only if nobody offers within the ceiling, cheapest first: in a hot market the poster pays over its max rather than leave the load.
  • A load nobody takes rolls to the next round with its ceiling up 10% and its appointment moved back a round. Nobody but a carrier ever hauls it: it rolls until a truck takes it or, after 10 rounds, the shipper gives up and the freight is lost. Open loads piling up where trucks are scarce lift that market's load-to-truck ratio, and so every new rate posted there.

Bidding by hand

  • Pick an idle truck and a load up to 300 miles away with the right trailer. One bid per truck per round; you can rebid or withdraw until it clears.
  • A hand bid replaces your dispatcher's bids for that truck that round.
  • The board shows your break-even for each load: fuel, wages, maintenance, insurance, expected repairs, the lessor's cut, the empty miles to the pickup, and the empty miles you'll likely run after delivering (reload odds).

Posters pay a 3% board fee on what the freight pays them. Carriers pay nothing to bid.

Book-now prices

Every load shows a book-now price: what the poster is offering right now. Take it and the load is yours at once, no round to wait for. Want more? Counter above it and wait for the round: the poster takes the best counter within its ceiling, or, if none is within it, the cheapest above (The load board). Anyone who'd take the posted price just books.

How posters set it

  • It opens as a share of the reference rate set by the load-to-truck ratio at the origin: about 88% where trucks pile up (ratio 0.5), 98% at a balanced 1, 110% when trucks are scarce (ratio 2), never above the ceiling.
  • Each round a load sits unfilled the poster raises it 12%, up to the ceiling (which rises 10% a round). Brokers fish: they open low where they can and pay up when nobody bites.
  • So a carrier that can wait makes the poster come up, and a carrier that has to move takes what's posted. In a truck-heavy market the book-now is cheap and rising; in a tight one it opens near the ceiling.

Your dispatcher and book-now

The standing order "Book loads between rounds" takes a load whenever its book-now clears your break-even plus a margin. The margin shrinks with desperation: a truck idle 24 hours, or a driver more than 14 days from home, will take anything that covers costs. A truck sitting at home is half as pressed. Dispatchers look every 2 hours.

A load shows its shipper's town and its receiver's town; its market (the board it trades on, the rate it's priced from) is the shipper's. Trucks drive the real roads between the two, so a load out of Homestead or Miles City takes you there.

Shipper leverage and contracts

Where trucks pile up, shippers hold the cards and work with carriers based nearby; where trucks are scarce, they take anyone. Each market holds a share of its loads for local carriers only (based within 150 miles) for the first round: 50% when loads and trucks balance, about 90% where trucks pile up (Miami most of the year), about 10% where they're scarce. A local-first load nobody local takes opens to everyone the next round. The join screen shows each hub's share.

Contracts

  • Every week local shippers offer contract lanes to the carriers based nearby with the best record: a weekly volume (1–3 loads) on a lane at a fixed rate for 91 days. The rate is the market's trailing contract level, the same brokers get: below spot in a tight season, above it in a slump. You can hold 2 at a time; offers stand 7 days.
  • A tender is a load on that lane held for you for 12 hours at the contract rate. Accept it onto an idle truck within reach, or leave it: it goes to the spot board, where you can even counter for it at spot.
  • Acceptance is what the shipper watches. Below 85% after 4 tenders they cut a load a week, and local shippers pass you over for 30 days. At 90% or better the term renews at the then-current rate.
  • The dispatcher takes offers and honors tenders unless you tell it to let a tender lapse when spot runs a set share above the contract. That's the produce-season choice: keep your customers, or chase the spike.

How shippers price freight

NPC shippers post at a reference rate. It starts from the lane's base rate: 2.45 FD/mi for a dry van, plus 120 FD ÷ miles on short hauls. Then it's multiplied by:

  • Tightness: the load-to-truck ratio within 150 miles of the origin for that trailer type, to the power 0.35 (kept within 0.5–2.5). Idle trucks that wouldn't take today's rates don't count as supply.
  • Weather at the origin.
  • Trailer: reefer ×1.18, flatbed ×1.12.
  • Season at the origin (Seasons).
  • Destination: freight into a market where trucks pile up pays more (reload odds).

A fuel surcharge passes on 80% of diesel's move above 4.00 FD/gal (at 6.5 mpg). The ceiling is the reference rate plus 15%. Most loads clear somewhere between the two.

Headhaul, backhaul and reload odds

Some markets consume far more than they ship. Miami takes in plenty and sends little out, so trucks pile up there and wait. Freight into Miami is the headhaul and pays well; freight out is the backhaul and barely pays. Producing markets work the other way.

Reload odds combine a market's balance for each trailer type with how tight it has been lately against the national average, smoothed over about a week. Every load shows its destination's odds:

LabelOddsMeans

Expected empty miles after delivery are 60 ÷ the odds, between 20 and 800. The destination's price factor is odds−0.2, within ×0.8–1.3. A cheap load into a good market can beat a rich one into a dead end.

Seasons

Every market has its own freight season, from the regional curves on beagl.ai's seasonality map: a slow build, a peak, a steep fall, then a dead zone. Each hub follows the nearest market's curve for dry van and reefer; flatbed follows the van curve at 80% strength.

  • South Florida produce builds slowly from winter, runs hot from late April through May, falls steeply in June and stays dead into autumn.
  • Georgia and the Carolinas pick up in June as Florida fades; the West and North run later in summer and fall.
  • Dry van and flatbed rise with produce season too, and northern markets tighten in winter.
  • Late December volume drops to 80% for the holidays.

A season moves freight volume (×0.5–2.5) and the posted rate (×0.7–1.6), and more freight tightens the market on top. The edge is timing: be where the season is going before the rates show it. The Company tab's Seasons now card shows the hottest and coldest markets; each load shows its origin's season.

Booms, busts and stabilizers

National freight demand follows a Cass-style cycle. The Company tab shows the demand dial: above 1.1 is a boom (more freight, higher rates), below 0.9 a slump. Booms invite everyone to buy trucks; the trucks arrive after the dealer's lead time, often as the market turns.

Published stabilizers keep the money supply in check. Each month NPC freight volume moves up to 10% (within ×0.75–1.5) and the diesel rack spread up to 1% (within ±5%), toward FD sinks matching faucets. After the token launches the profit tax moves first (The token).

Fuel

Diesel starts at 4.00 FD a gallon and follows real diesel prices at half strength, within ±25%, times the rack spread. Your fuel bill is miles ÷ mpg × price: a Workhorse gets 6.5 mpg, a Highway Pro 7.2, a Flagship 7.8. Wear costs up to 20% of that, million-mile trucks gain up to 4%, and your driver moves it ±15%. The fuel surcharge on every load passes most of a price spike to shippers.

Brokers and contracts

About 50% of NPC freight is tendered to AI brokers at contract rates, which trail the spot market by about 30 days. A broker reposts the load at its margin (typically 6–12%) and pays the carrier whatever the shipper does. In a spike, brokers pay carriers more than their contracts bring in; in a slide, the contracts pad their margin.

Some shippers don't pay their brokers (about 2% of loads, more in a slump). The broker still pays its carrier. Brokers choose which carriers they'll load: see Vetting.

Running trucks

You against your dispatcher

Standing orders are your dispatcher's rules. Whoever dispatches (you as owner, plus any dispatchers you hire) runs them every round for every idle truck you didn't bid on by hand. Their skill decides how well:

  • Pricing: they misjudge their own costs by about 8% when green and 1% at their best. The loads they win are disproportionately the ones they underpriced.
  • Reload awareness: they price in 0.7× to 1.2× of the empty run out of a destination.
  • Foresight: they look 0 to 42 days ahead on each market's season when pricing loads and moving idle trucks.
  • Span: you can run 3 trucks; a hired dispatcher 5–18. Past the team's span, some idle trucks sit out each round.

You can always beat the best of them by hand. You see everything they see: exact ceilings, reload odds, every market's season a year out, and where the AI is crowding. You aren't held to their rule set or their mistakes. The Staff tab shows your team's numbers.

Hours, dock time and delivery windows

  • A driver gets 11 driving hours in a 24-hour duty day that opens when the truck first rolls: about 550 miles at 50 mph (your driver's skill moves speed ±5%). A team of two drivers gets 22.
  • Loading and unloading take 1–2 hours each, on duty but not driving.
  • A load is due after the drive at the legal pace, the worst-case dock time and 12 hours of slack. 12 hours early or more counts as early; past due is late.
  • A breakdown stops the truck 6–18 hours and can make it late.

Hot loads

About 4% of long-haul freight (700 miles or more) is hot: it pays 1.6–2.2× for a window that assumes 15 driving hours a day with only 2 hours of slack. A solo driver can't make it legally; a team can. Delivered late, a hot load pays only 50% of its premium. Hot loads are at least standard-vetted.

Roads, tolls and weather

Every lane runs on the real road network: the interstates and the US highways, drawn on the map, with each route the fastest way between its two hubs at interstate speed (58 mph) or two-lane highway speed (42 mph). Trucks move along the road on the map, and weigh stations sit on the roads a route actually takes.

  • Tolls. Toll roads (the Pennsylvania Turnpike, the Ohio and Indiana toll roads, the New Jersey Turnpike, Florida's Turnpike, the Kansas and Oklahoma turnpikes, the New York Thruway and others) charge a truck by the mile at their real rates. Routing weighs a toll against the time it saves, so a free road wins when it's nearly as quick. Tolls are paid as the miles are driven, the load board shows them on every load, and your break-even counts them. Shippers pay toll routes a little more, the way they pay a fuel surcharge.
  • Two-lane highway. A stretch of US highway is slower and 1.6× as likely to see a crash as an interstate. The load board flags loads whose route runs on it.
  • Winter. From December to February, crash odds climb with latitude north of 36°: up to +35% on interstates and +90% on highways at the Canadian border. A Minneapolis run in January is not a Houston run in June.
  • Hours. A load's delivery window comes from its route's own driving time, not a flat speed, so a two-lane lane allows more time than an interstate lane of the same length.

Reputation and on-time

Your reputation is your smoothed on-time rate: a new company counts as 10 loads at 85%, then every delivery moves it. It's 25% of every bid's score, so a good record wins loads at higher rates.

Your level is the square root of loads delivered ÷ 5, up to 20, and it multiplies the Miles you earn (×1–1.5). Going broke resets both.

Wear, breakdowns and rebuilds

  • An owned truck's condition falls from 100% to 0 over 200,000 miles since its last rebuild. Fully worn, it burns 25% more fuel and doubles maintenance.
  • Breakdowns: a new Workhorse about once every 66,667 miles, ×(1 + 8·wear²) as it wears and more as it ages; a Highway Pro breaks down 30% less, a Flagship 50% less. Each costs 1,500–4,500 FD on an owned truck; the lessor pays on a leased one. A skilled driver means fewer.
  • Rebuild an idle owned truck at 85% condition or worse: 2 days in the shop, back to 100%. It costs 10,000 FD for a Workhorse (14,000 Highway Pro, 19,000 Flagship), more the higher the odometer: double at 500,000 miles.
  • A worn truck's break-even is higher, so it loses bids to fresher trucks. That, not a rule, is the reason to rebuild.
  • Million-mile trucks: a level every 50,000 miles, up to 20; every five levels adds 1% fuel economy, up to 4%.
  • Defects (worn brakes, tires, lights) build up slowly on any truck, faster if maintenance is skipped. They raise breakdown and crash odds and get trucks put out of service at inspections. Fix them from the Fleet tab for 350 FD each.

Staff

Drivers

Every truck needs a driver; a truck without one sits. You drive your first truck yourself, at a fixed owner's draw of 0.75 FD/mi, and you never quit on yourself. Every other seat is a hire.

Speed moves ±5% with skill too. Market pay is multiplied by the wage index, which rises up to ×1.15 when the pool of unemployed drivers runs thin and falls to ×0.92 when it's deep.

Experience: each mile closes a little of the gap between a driver's skill and their own potential (gap × miles ÷ 300,000). A rookie with promise becomes a veteran in a year or two of steady driving, and from then on the market will pay them like one.

Teams: seat a co-driver on a truck that already has a driver and it runs 22 hours a day legally. Each driver earns 60% of their rate on every mile, so a team costs about 20% more per mile than one driver and covers twice the ground. Teams are how hot loads get delivered legally.

Every driver carries a record of violations and crashes that any employer can see before hiring.

Fatigue, home time and per diem

Drivers wear down on the road. Each one has a fatigue level you can see on the Staff tab and on their truck.

  • Weeks out: past 21 consecutive days away from the home hub, fatigue builds every day, faster the longer they're out.
  • Long days: a day over 10 driving hours builds it too, and each consecutive long day builds more than the last. Running over hours on falsified logs makes long days routine.
  • Days off: 5 straight days off at home reset fatigue to zero. Each extra day off banks a buffer (up to 3 days' worth) that absorbs the next stretch before fatigue starts climbing. A day off on the road takes a little off but doesn't reset the clock.
  • What it does: crash odds ×3 at full fatigue, the truck runs up to 10% slower, and a tired hire's morale drops every day until they quit.

Home time

The standing order "Bring drivers home" brings a truck home after a set number of days out and rests its driver for a set number of days; the dispatcher leaves the truck alone meanwhile. A truck due home keeps hauling, but only loads that deliver near home or at least 100 miles closer to it; it runs empty the last 400 miles, or after 2 days without a homeward load. "Send home" on a truck's card runs it straight home. Teams rest together. You drive your first truck yourself, and the same rules apply to you.

Per diem

Every day a truck sits idle away from the home hub costs the company 30 FD in per diem (a layover: the hotel, the parking, the meals). On the road, wages cover it. A truck waiting around far from home is spending money and wearing its driver out; that's why a local carrier can wait for a good load and a visitor can't.

Hiring, poaching and keeping people

  • Hiring: anyone in the pool takes a job at their asking pay or more: market pay for their skill, times a personal premium of -5% to 8%. The pool refills every week with rookies and returning veterans.
  • Morale drifts each week toward a target set mostly by pay against market (every 10% under costs about 20%), plus up to 15% for tenure, minus 10% for a worn-out truck, 10% for a conditional or unsatisfactory safety rating, and up to 35% for running outside the rules, which the best drivers mind most.
  • Quitting: settled people rarely leave; unhappy ones quit at up to about 5% a week. A driver left without a truck walks after 2 weeks. Anyone mid-trip finishes the load first.
  • Poaching: anyone working elsewhere will move for a raise of 4% (miserable) to 20% (happy) over what they make, and at least their asking pay. Their employer gets 2 days to match. If they match, they stay.
  • Your people get offers too. The Staff tab flags them with a Match button. Raises keep people; a cut lowers morale on the spot.
  • Going broke or losing your authority lets everyone go.

Auto-staff

On by default. Each day it seats any idle driver on a truck that needs one, hires the best-value driver in the pool for any remaining empty seat (asking pay against what their skill saves), hires a dispatcher when your fleet outgrows your team's span, and lets go a dispatcher you no longer need. It doesn't give raises, match offers, or poach: those are yours. Turn it off on the Staff tab to do it all by hand.

Growing the company

Choosing a home hub

You pick a home hub when you start: one of the 10 starter hubs. Your trucks start there, new and leased trucks are delivered there, and it sets three costs and one edge.

  • Local edge: shippers favor carriers with a yard nearby. Loads from within 150 miles of your home score 4 points higher (out of 100) when your hub has its fair share of carriers, up to 8 when it's wide open and as little as 1 when it's crowded.
  • Crowding is the carriers based at a hub against its fair share of all carriers by freight volume. Chicago has the most freight, and it can also have the most competition.
  • Yard rent: 20 FD a week for each truck past your first × the metro factor (0.6 + 0.3 × the hub's freight size) × crowding (within ×0.6–2). Parked trucks pay it too.
  • Insurance garaging: premiums follow each state's lawsuit climate. See Insurance.

Leasing

  • Your first truck is an NPC lease: a Workhorse with a dry van. The lessor takes 15% of every load's pay and pays for major repairs.
  • Extra leases, once you own a truck: any trailer, no money down, the same 15%, at least 250 FD a week, a 28-day minimum term, up to 10 at a time. You hire a driver for each.
  • The lessor's fleet grows when nearly all of it is out and freight is going uncovered, and shrinks when leases come back, so in a boom you may find none left.

Prices move

Each tractor tier and trailer type has its own price index. Every month it moves a third of the way toward a target: up 40% as the dealer's backlog doubles the wait, up as supply runs short (fewer than 15% of the cap left) and down when there's plenty, within ×0.85–1.3 of list. Used values move with new prices. Buying into a boom costs more.

Delivery and supply

  • You pay when you order. The truck arrives 30 days later, plus a day for every 4 trucks already on order nationally (up to 150). Orders placed in a boom often land as it turns.
  • Supply is capped: 1,200 Workhorses, 500 Highway Pros and 100 Flagships in the whole world. 35% of Workhorses and dry vans are held for companies buying their first truck.
  • Ordering a first truck while on the NPC lease replaces the lease when it arrives. A company can run up to 30 trucks.

The used market

List an idle owned truck at an asking price, a floor, and a weekly price cut; a buyer gets it today, where it stands. The seller gets 95% and 5% is burned.

A truck's value falls with mileage (to 12% of new at 1,000,000 miles), 5% a year with age, and up to 40% more for wear, all against what the same truck costs new today.

Equipment Finance

  • Borrow up to 35% of an owned truck's value at 1% a week, repaid over 26 weeks.
  • Miss 2 payments and the truck is repossessed and sold; depositors absorb any shortfall.
  • Deposit spare FD to earn the interest borrowers pay. Idle cash in the pool pays the holding cost like any other.

Money

Where FD comes from and goes

Freight Dollars (FD) are the game's money. Every FD movement is double-entry: it comes from somewhere and goes somewhere.

  • In: NPC shippers paying for freight, the NPC market buying facility goods, starting grants, and (after launch) FD bought with dollars.
  • Out (burned): fuel, wages, maintenance and repairs, insurance, yard rent, overhead, dispatcher pay, lease shares, the dealer, fines and judgments, board fees, the holding cost, the profit tax after launch.
  • Between companies: brokers and shippers paying carriers, used-truck sales, loans and deposits.

The Company tab's market report shows the freight cycle and rates; stabilizers keep what's burned close to what's created (Booms, busts and stabilizers).

Insurance

Every company holds a one-game-year policy, priced when it's signed and fixed for the term, billed weekly for each truck on the road. It renews at the then-current price.

Price per truck per year = 4,200 FD × your record × your home hub's garaging factor × the insurance market.

  • Dispatchers spread the premium over a typical truck's 52,500 loaded miles a year when they price loads.

The holding cost

Cash isn't free to sit on. Each week 2% of any cash above your working capital is burned. Working capital is the larger of 25,000 FD and what you spent in the last four weeks, so a buffer for a bust is free and a hoard isn't. Put spare FD to work: trucks, facilities, or Equipment Finance loans to others.

Going broke

  • More than 2,000 FD in the red at 4 weekly checks in a row and the company is liquidated: bidding stops, staff are let go, owned trucks are fire-sold (from 70% of value, down 10% a week), facilities close, and extra leases go back.
  • A judgment bigger than the company can pay winds it up the same way.
  • Then you start over on the NPC lease with whatever is left, no grant. Debts are written off, your reputation starts fresh, and any airdrop not yet unlocked is forfeited.

Miles

Miles are points for hauling well, and they become the airdrop at launch. Each delivered load earns its loaded miles ×:

  • On time: early (12+ hours) ×1.2, on time ×1, late ×0.5;
  • Margin: your margin ÷ 25%, within ×0.5–1.2;
  • Reputation level: ×1–1.5;
  • Every truck counts the same: Miles are flat per loaded mile, however many trucks you run.

No Miles for a load that cleared more than 40% off its reference rate (a hot load's premium counts as its reference). At most 3,000 Miles a day per company, and only the first 3 loads a day on the same lane count. Brokers earn 0.15 per mile brokered.

The token

Beta

The first 2 game years are the beta: FD is play money and Miles pile up.

Launch

  • FD above each company's working capital is burned; what's kept is locked: it can be spent in the game, not sold.
  • Stakes, bonds and contract stakes are beta FD too: they are burned with the rest, and every licence gets its grace period to be re-posted with real FD.
  • An FD/USDC pool opens with the farming season's dollars on one side and the pool allocation's FD on the other, as deep as the FD that survives the reset: the launch price is what those dollars support.
  • Airdrop: 10,000,000 FD shared by Miles, at most 1% to any one company. 10% unlocks at launch; the rest over 26 weeks, a slice each week you handle at least 3 loads (a missed week's slice is forfeited). Or claim the rest at once for 50% of it.

After launch

  • Buy FD with dollars (1% fee) or sell unlocked FD (7% fee, burned) on the Money tab.
  • Profit tax: 20% of each week's operating profit above 5,000 FD is burned. The rate moves 5% a month within 10%–30% to keep the money supply steady.
  • Half of the game's revenue buys FD back from the pool and burns it.

Safety and the law

Your safety record

Like a real carrier, your company is scored in four areas. Violations count three times as much for six months, twice as much for the next six, once more for the year after, then drop off. Inspections with nothing found dilute the score.

AreaAlert at
Hours of service5
Unsafe driving4
Vehicle maintenance6
Crashes (per truck)1.5

An alert gets your trucks pulled into more weigh stations, raises insurance at renewal, keeps strict brokers away, and two at once call in an auditor.

Weigh stations and inspections

  • Trucks pass weigh stations on the main interstate corridors, at about the real locations. A station is open 60% of the time. Clean carriers are waved through most of the time; alerts, a poor rating or a brand-new authority get pulled in far more.
  • Police make traffic stops on the road, more often for trucks that are speeding.
  • An inspection checks the driver and logs, the truck, or both. Problems found mean fines, violations on your record, and sometimes an out-of-service order on the spot: the driver must rest, or the truck is repaired at roadside prices before it moves.
  • Defects build up on every truck, slowly with full maintenance. Fix them from the Fleet tab before an inspector finds them.

Every inspection, clean or not, shows up in your ticker.

Compliance reviews and ratings

  • A new authority gets a new-entrant audit at 90 days. After that, reviews follow two alerts at once (at most every 180 days), an at-fault crash with injuries or deaths, or a scheduled follow-up.
  • An auditor goes through your records, fines what turns up (capped at 60,000 FD a review), and rates you satisfactory, conditional or unsatisfactory.
  • Conditional: insurance ×1.5 at once, strict brokers won't load you, more inspections, and a follow-up in 90 days that can clear it.
  • Unsatisfactory: insurance ×2.5 at once, most brokers won't load you, and 45 days to clean up. Still unsatisfactory at the follow-up and your authority is revoked (Shutdowns).
  • Bad ratings also cost you people: drivers don't like working for a carrier with a poor rating.

Crashes

A typical legal truck crashes about once every 3,333,333 miles. Fatigue, speed, defects, wear and a green driver all raise the odds; a veteran driver lowers them. About 3% of crashes kill someone and 25% injure someone; fatigue or speed makes them deadlier.

What a crash costs depends on why it happened:

CauseWhat follows
Not at faultThe other side's insurer pays. You lose the load and days in the shop.
At fault, nothing wrongYour insurer pays up to 1,000,000 FD. You pay the 5,000 FD deductible and anything over the limit; premiums jump at renewal (or at once, for injuries and deaths); a crash on your record.
At fault, with violations behind itThe same, but juries multiply verdicts 2–4×. A verdict the company can't pay winds it up.
Fatal, caused by falsified hours or known defectsA crime. Authority revoked, everything the company has seized, the owner barred for 365 days, and the people who ran the truck that way jailed.

A fatal crash usually totals the truck; a leased one costs 6,000 FD owed to the lessor. A crash with cargo aboard loses the load and its pay, and you pay the cargo deductible.

Shutdowns

A revoked authority means the fleet is sold off, the staff are let go, Miles and any locked airdrop are forfeited, and the account can't file for new authority until its bar runs out:

CauseBar
Unsatisfactory at the follow-up review30 days
A second revocation180 days
A crime365 days, everything seized
2 crimesBarred for good

When the bar is over, the account gets new authority and starts over on a lease. Brokers can see the old revocation for a year.

Vetting

Brokers and shippers choose who may haul their freight. Every load shows its poster's requirement:

TierRequires
OpenAnyone
StandardAuthority at least 30 days old, no open claims, not rated unsatisfactory
StrictAll of the above, plus at least 90 days and 25 loads delivered (or a year old), no conditional rating or alerts, no revocation in the past year; tracking on the load

Hot loads are at least standard. A new carrier starts on open freight and earns its way up. Open freight draws more bidders, so it tends to clear cheaper.

Questions

Why did I lose a bid at a lower rate than the winner? Price is 55% of the score; reputation, empty miles and the home-market edge make up the rest.

Why are my trucks sitting idle? No freight in range that clears your floor, no driver, or your fleet is past your dispatch team's span (the Staff tab says which).

Why did my insurance go up? A renewal re-prices it: check the Company tab for your record, garaging and the market. A serious crash or a bad rating re-writes it at once.

Can I pause the live world? No. Only test worlds pause. See The live world and test worlds.

Is a hired dispatcher better than me? Never better than your own bids by hand. See You against your dispatcher.
